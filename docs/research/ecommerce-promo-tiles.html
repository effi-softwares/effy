Field research · merchandising surfaces · August 2026

How the big retailers actually build promo tiles

Thirteen grocery, food-delivery and general-retail platforms, examined through their delivered markup, embedded payloads and shipped JavaScript rather than their screenshots. The short version: the copy is live DOM text almost everywhere, the artwork is commissioned per tile shape rather than cropped, and most of them solve text-over-photography by not putting text on the photograph.

The questions you asked, answered first

Three direct answers

1. Is the tile's text live DOM text, or baked into the image?

Live DOM text at ten of the twelve surfaces where evidence was obtained. Two exceptions, both instructive: Amazon bakes everything, and Woolworths bakes exactly one thing on purpose.

There is a reliable forensic tell, and it works on any site: look at the alt attribute.

  • When the copy is live text, the artwork is decorative and the alt is empty or descriptive of the picture. Walmart: alt="Black backpack on a wood surface next to a banana, snacks and a travel cup." — that describes a photograph, not an offer. eBay goes further and wraps the image in role="img" aria-hidden="true" with an empty alt. Uber Eats' offer badge uses <img alt="" role="presentation"> for the trophy icon beside live text.
  • When the copy is baked into the pixels, the alt carries the marketing copy, because it is the only place the words can live. Target's Superhero: alt="College, Dorm deals are here, bullseye university" — and the component's text container next to it is rendered empty. Amazon, the fully-baked outlier, duplicates its entire headline into both alt and aria-label.

Target does both deliberately: its ultra-wide Superhero is a flat Scene7 composite with the words in the artwork, while its Story Block tiles use live <h2>/<h3> text. Baked-in text is a per-slot choice reserved for one full-bleed placement, not a house position.

The most useful negative result: Uber Eats proves it by paired comparison

One captured document contains two adjacent store tiles. "Rocket Sushi" carries "promotion":{"text":" Top Offer • Buy 1, Get 1 Free","promotionUuid":"…"} and renders a badge div; "Nojo Ramen Tavern" carries "promotion":null and its <figure> holds only a lazy-load placeholder. Both have the identical six-rung image ladder. The image pipeline is the same and only the data differs — so the offer cannot be in the image. In the captured markup the badge text is a sibling of an image container that has not even loaded yet. And the badge model carries textColor:{color:"#FFFFFF"} at runtime, which is impossible on baked pixels.

Woolworths bakes exactly one field, and does it correctly

The only principled hybrid in the sample: the price goes into the artwork — so a designer controls its typography, which is the one genuine argument for baking — while headline, subcopy and CTA stay live. It is made accessible by a parallel sr-only restatement of the price in the DOM, never left as pixels alone. If you ever bake anything, this is the shape: one field, for a typographic reason, with a text restatement beside it.

2. What makes text legible over photography without ruining the photograph?

The dominant answer across these sites is not a gradient. It is don't put the text on the photograph. Five of the seven surfaces I could read place their promo copy either beside the image in a colour panel or below it entirely, as the default state. The full ranking, with evidence, is further down.

The most telling single data point: Shopify's Dawn theme — the reference storefront implementation, used by millions of shops — ships image_overlay_opacity with a default of 0, show_text_box defaulting to true, and show_text_below defaulting to true on mobile. Its own CSS then does .banner--mobile-bottom::after { display: none }: when the text leaves the image, the scrim is deleted. The scrim exists only to serve the text, never as a look.

3. Is "a discount code with rules" the same entity as "editorial merchandising that links somewhere"?

Two entities, everywhere I could observe both in one payload. The banner never carries the discount's rules; it carries a destination and, at most, a human-readable summary plus a link to terms.

  • Instacart is the cleanest proof, because both live in the same GraphQL cache. Editorial placements are ContentManagement* types — a placement id, a placement type, a placement layout, and a named data query. The offer is an entirely separate ItemsResponseBackedItemPriceItemPromotionSection carrying offerLabelString ("Buy 2 or more for $4.60 each"), offerSublabelString, offerReferenceString, badgeVariant and clip-coupon state. Different type, different lifecycle, attached to an item, not to a banner.
  • eBay's banner headline says "Best loved items at 12%* off" as plain marketing copy; the CTA links to a campaign landing page and a separate small link labelled *See terms. points at pages.ebay.com/promo/2026/his12aug/. The banner holds no code and no rules.
  • Walmart's card schema has a legalDisclosure field and a primaryCta.clickThrough URL — but nothing resembling a discount rule, threshold or code. Headings routinely end in an asterisk ("Free setup & support on select computers*") whose expansion lives elsewhere.

What that means for a platform with a promo-code table

The banner should reference a promotion, not embed it. Everything the shopper reads on the tile — eyebrow, headline, terms line — is authored copy belonging to the banner; the code, thresholds, caps and window belong to the promotion, and the tile's only structural link to it is a destination URL. This is exactly why a cart-level code has no products to filter to, and why a promotion tap needs a destination that restates the offer rather than a search result.

1

Text entirely outside the artwork — below it, or beside it

Image is a clean rectangle with its own aspect ratio; headline, subcopy and CTA are ordinary flow content underneath or in an adjacent column. Nothing overlaps, so nothing can fail.

Evidence: Aldi AU — all ten cms-multilayout-teaser blocks put __title and __description in a sibling __content div after the <picture>. Instacart — hero <h1> and the three value-prop cards keep copy out of the image; the decorative PNG carries alt="". Target Story Blocks — styles_storycardTextDefaultPosition renders <h2>/<h3> below the picture. Shopify Dawn — show_text_below defaults to true.

2

A flat authored colour area — block background, text box, or headline background

The merchandiser picks a colour, and the type sits on that colour rather than on pixels. Sometimes the whole module has a background; sometimes only the copy does; sometimes only on mobile.

Evidence: Target ships three separate levers — a module-level background-color (eleven blocks on #FAF0E6, plus #97C3BC, #D6E8E6, #FFAF99, #516680), a --headline-bg-color used with five distinct values, and a --mobile-copy-background-color used on four blocks specifically for the mobile breakpoint. Shopify Dawn — show_text_box defaults true, and the box takes a named color_scheme. eBay — dp-banner-color-theme__container. Aldi — every teaser carries an inline background-color.

3

Per-tile authored text colour over artwork commissioned with a clear area

No overlay at all. The merchandiser sets the type colour to suit the specific photograph, and the photograph is briefed and shot with an empty region where the words go. The schema has a colour field and no scrim field.

Evidence: Walmart — every card carries heading.textColor, eyebrow.textColor and primaryCtaGroup.textColor as authored hex. The full-width hero uses #ffffff; the five collection tiles all use Walmart navy #001e60 over light-ground photography. There is no overlay, opacity or gradient field anywhere in the card schema. Target's asset filenames encode the same discipline — the slot is named in the file (…-Col-Hero.jpg, …-Col-VerticalSm.jpg).

4

Copy baked into the image

Reserved for one full-bleed slot where the typography is part of the campaign artwork. Costs you translation, responsive text sizing, selectable text and search — and it forces the marketing copy into the alt attribute.

Evidence: Target's Superhero only. Two Scene7 assets, 2400×473 desktop and 1500×647 mobile, with the component's text container rendered empty and the headline living in alt. Its Story Blocks, in the same page, use live text — so this is a per-slot choice, not a platform position.

5

An overlay scrim — the minority, and never left to the client to guess

Two platforms use one as standard, and neither hard-codes it. IKEA's is a fixed-height band anchored to the bottom, not a full-tile wash, with thirteen colour stops on an eased falloff so there is no perceptible edge. Uber Eats makes it a server-supplied number.

Evidence: IKEA — all thirteen hri-text-overlay-card instances render a dedicated <div class="hri-text-overlay-card__gradient"></div> as the first child of the card's link. Uber Eats — its shipped billboard renderer destructures image = {url, overlayOpacity, responsiveImagesByFormat} alongside card = {backgroundColor, textColor, isBackgroundDark, …}. Shopify Dawn offers a scrim but it is a flat #000000 layer at author-set opacity, defaulting to 0.

The only published specification of any of this — and it is a grocery one

Instacart's retailer-facing storefront documentation states the primary banner's composition as an explicit ratio: desktop “56% solid color with 44% visible image”; mobile “65% solid color with 35% visible image”, with text and button on the left. That is techniques 1 and 2 fused into a content contract — the merchandiser is told, in percentages, how much of the tile is a flat colour panel, and the proportion grows on mobile because the copy needs proportionally more room. It is the single most directly usable number in this research, and I found nothing else like it published anywhere.

Why the thirteen stops matter

A two-stop linear-gradient(transparent, rgba(0,0,0,.6)) interpolates linearly in sRGB, which the eye reads as a soft ramp that abruptly stops — you can see the line where it terminates, and it flattens the mid-tones across the whole lower half. The eased multi-stop curve concentrates almost all of the darkening in the bottom fifth and tapers to 1% before it ends, so there is no edge to see and the photograph's mid-tones survive. If you take one implementation detail from this research, take this one.

isBackgroundDark — the field that solves the problem an inverting accent creates

Uber Eats' card model carries a boolean, isBackgroundDark, next to textColor and overlayOpacity. The tile's type colour is chosen from the artwork's luminance, decided server-side when the creative is authored — not from the viewer's theme.

That distinction is the whole point. A photograph does not invert when the UI does. A design system whose accent flips between light and dark will, if the tile naively takes its colour from the theme, put dark type on a dark photo in one appearance and white type on a bright one in the other — and the artwork looks identical in both. Uber's answer is to make the tile's foreground a property of the image, carried in the payload, and to leave it alone when the theme changes. The same reasoning is why IKEA's scrim is fixed dark in both appearances: the thing that makes type legible over an image cannot be the thing that inverts.

What you have to hit, and against what

Nielsen Norman Group's guidance on text over images sets the bar at WCAG's 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (18pt, or 14pt bold). Their governing principle is the part teams skip, and it is the reason a scrim tuned against one hero fails against the next one:

Ensure the method provides a high enough contrast for the worst-case background image and text placement.

This is what makes techniques 1 and 2 structurally safer than 3 and 5: a flat colour panel has one contrast ratio that can be machine-checked once, while an overlay's effective contrast depends on a photograph nobody has uploaded yet. It is also the argument for keeping a scrim's strength as a two-value variant — IKEA's 54% and 65% — rather than a free slider.

The industry-standard promotional tile

Every field below appears in at least two of the four systems I could read schemas for. Where the systems agree on a name, I have used theirs.

Copy

  • eyebrow optional — the kicker above the headline. Walmart eyebrow.text; Instacart has it as a distinct string per section.
  • headline required — Walmart heading.text, Target <h2>, Aldi __title, Dawn heading block.
  • subcopy optional — Walmart subheading, Target <h3>, Aldi __description, Dawn text block.
  • disclaimer optional — Walmart legalDisclosure, Instacart legalCopyString, eBay's clamped terms line. Frequently carries its own URL.

Action

  • ctaLabel — Walmart primaryCta.linkText, Aldi base-button__label, Dawn button_label_1.
  • ctaHref — Walmart primaryCta.clickThrough.{type,value}. Note type: the destination is a tagged union, not a bare URL.
  • ctaStyle — Walmart primaryCtaStyle: "pill" | "textLink"; Dawn button_style_secondary. Two variants, no more.
  • ctaHidden — Walmart ships both enableCta and isCtaHidden; the whole tile stays clickable either way.

Artwork

  • image.src + image.assetId — the asset is identified, not just linked.
  • image.alt — always present, usually empty. Walmart writes "" on the hero and a description on the rest.
  • image.templateUrl — Instacart's …/{width=}x{height=}/…. The client fills in dimensions; the CDN renders.
  • mobileImage optional — Instacart's mobileBackgroundImage, Target's second Scene7 asset. A genuinely different composition, not a crop.

Presentation

  • backgroundColor — on every Walmart card and every Aldi teaser.
  • textColor — Walmart authors it per copy field; eBay and Dawn instead reference a named colour scheme. The named-scheme approach is the one that survives a design system.
  • contentPosition — Dawn's nine-cell grid (top-left…bottom-right); Walmart's verticalAlignment+horizontalAlignment; Target's per-breakpoint percentage coordinates.
  • aspectRatio — per breakpoint. Target authors decimals derived from the uploaded asset; IKEA uses named tokens (square-to-semiportrait-from-lg).

Badge

  • badge.text + badge.variant — IKEA's commercial-message component with variants family and new; Instacart's badgeVariant: "retailerPromotion".
  • Target instead exposes raw styling — --ribbon-background-color, --ribbon-font-color, --ribbon-padding. The variant approach ages better than the hex approach.

Placement & lifecycle

  • placementId / zone — Walmart binds each module to a contentZone{N}; Instacart carries cm_placement_id, cm_placement_type, cm_placement_layout.
  • position — sparse integers. Target's slots run 200, 400, 425, 450, 460, 475, 500, 525, 600… Gaps exist so a merchandiser can insert without renumbering.
  • status + version + publishedAt — Walmart carries all three on every module (version: 9, status: "published").
  • name — a human campaign label. Target: HP_DormDeals_SH_0809. Walmart: Nonmember 8/3 7AM PST - 8/10 7AM PST - Full Width HeroPOV - Desktop. The scheduling window lives in the name because a human needs to recognise it in a list.
  • trackingProperties — on every section, at every platform, without exception.

DoorDash argues for naming the slots semantically, not editorially

The field list above is editorial — eyebrow, headline, subcopy. DoorDash's published Facet model takes the opposite view and uses positional slots: title, subtitle, accessory, description for text, and main, icon, background, accessory for images, each documented as meaning "different things in the context of different components".

The trade is real. Semantic slots let one payload feed a hero, a rail tile and a list row without a new type per surface — which is why a company serving restaurants, grocery and convenience from one feed chose them. Editorial names make the authoring UI self-explanatory and the validation obvious. For a single-brand storefront with a handful of tile shapes, the editorial naming is almost certainly right; the DoorDash model earns its abstraction at a scale most platforms never reach. Their own post warns that the generic custom map became an anti-pattern engineers reached for instead of the real fields.

Three things I expected to find and did not

No scrim or overlay field in Walmart's, Target's or Instacart's schemas — only Dawn and Uber Eats have one, and Dawn defaults it off. No discount rules on any banner entity, at any platform. And no public design system documents a marketing promo component at all: eBay Evo has Card, Item Tile, Carousel, Badge and four kinds of notice, but no Banner, Hero or Promotion. Uber's Base Web does have a "Banner" — defined as "a contextual message… for inline callouts, form validation, and warning/success/failure states". In design-system vocabulary, "banner" means system alert, not promotion. If you name a component Banner, expect to disambiguate it forever.

Uber Eats' billboard card, lifted from its own shipped bundle

The closest thing to an independent replication of the Walmart schema, arrived at by a different company for a different vertical — and the field list is almost the same one.

card  = { backgroundImage, trailingImage, backgroundColor, textColor, isBackgroundDark,
          title, subtitle, cta { text, backgroundColor, textColor },
          eyebrowRichText, uuid, storeAd }
image = { url, overlayOpacity, responsiveImagesByFormat }   // a .json url means Lottie

// store-tile offer badge

feed card types: BILLBOARD | INFEED_BANNER | INFO_BANNER | BACKGROUND_IMAGE_CAROUSEL

Three details worth taking. eyebrowRichText confirms the eyebrow as a distinct field and as rich text, matching Target's WYSIWYG headline. The CTA carries its own backgroundColor/textColor independent of the card's — so a pill can be legible even where the headline sits on a different part of the image. And accessibilityText ships beside the visible badge text rather than replacing it, which is how you get "Top Offer, Buy one get one free" read aloud from a badge that visually reads "Top Offer • Buy 1, Get 1 Free".

Evidence grade: this is read from Uber's shipped renderer source and from live analytics payloads (a real April 2026 event carries "cardTitle":"¡Combo Classic Burger de Carl's Jr. a solo $149!" — a headline with a price travelling as a string field). No rendered billboard DOM was captured, so treat the field list as authoritative and the rendered markup as inferred.

The three findings that matter

  • Almost nobody branches on format in markup — but "nobody" would be wrong. Six of the seven Western retail sites emit no type="image/webp" at all: the URL advertises JPEG and the CDN negotiates. Target and Aldi both returned image/avif for a URL literally containing fmt=pjpeg. Uber Eats is the exception — it ships an explicit <picture><source type="image/webp"> and encodes the format into the URL path (/image-proc/resize/eats/format=webp/width=…/srcb64=<base64(originUrl)>). Both approaches ship in production; negotiation is the majority and needs no template branch, explicit type is the belt-and-braces minority.
  • Art direction is real, and it means a second asset — not a crop. Target's two hero masters measure 2400×473 (5.074:1) and 1500×647 (2.318:1), and those decimals appear exactly in the component's --aspect-ratio-lg and --aspect-ratio-default. The ratio is read off the uploaded file. Walmart goes further and commissions a distinct photograph per bento slot shape.
  • The alternative is a named server-side crop. Aldi uploads one 2:1 master and requests :2-1 or :16-9 from Scene7 per breakpoint. One asset, two shapes, no second commission — meaningfully cheaper to operate, and the crop is defined once in the DAM rather than in CSS.
Composition

The bento grid itself

Tile size is authored data at every platform I could read — never positional convention. The three systems solve it three different ways, in increasing order of rigidity.

Walmart — named slots
A module declares a viewType (collectionView, fiveCardGridHeroFullWidthView) and exposes one authored object per semantically named slot: collectionHero, horizontal, verticalSmallLeft, verticalSmallRight, verticalLarge. The merchandiser fills a shape, not an index. Page layout above that is a nested container tree with flow: vertical|horizontal, style.gridColumns, and slots mapping to ordered contentZone{N} refs.
Target — spans per breakpoint
A twelve-column grid where each block carries its own span as inline custom properties: --xs-col-b: calc(12/12*100%), --md-col-b: calc(6/12*100%). Carousels get --xs-col-p: calc(100%/3), --md-col-p: calc(100%/5), --lg-col-p: calc(100%/6) — three, five and six across. Every block is wrapped in a GenericSlingshotPagePlannerSlot carrying data-component-type, data-component-position, data-component-version and data-content-type.
IKEA — container queries
The most modern of the three, and the most relevant if you want one tile component at many sizes. .hri-bento-box-card declares container-type: inline-size; container-name: bento-box-card, and the tile's own contents respond to its width — the circular tag scales 100px→330px across four container breakpoints and hides itself below 250px. The tile never needs to know where it sits. IKEA also ships a hri-bento-box-card--text variant with no image at all.

The component taxonomy is small

Target's homepage rendered twenty page-planner slots drawing on just seven component types: Superhero, Story Block, Story, Banner, Manual TCIN Carousel, Item Or Promotion Carousel, Browse – Manual. Each slot also declares whether its contents are hand-picked or algorithmic — data-content-type was one of all_manual, manual, static, ct_static or dynamic.

Aspect ratios observed on Target: 1/1, 2/1, 2.149/1, 2.318/1, 3.641/1, 5.074/1, 5.53/1, 9.877/1. The odd decimals are not design decisions — they are the true dimensions of whatever the studio delivered.

The disagreement is about whether the whole tile is one link. The agreement is on four points, and they held without exception:

  • Never a link inside a link. Where the whole tile is clickable (IKEA, Target), there is exactly one <a> and the "CTA" is a visual affordance — IKEA's is an aria-hidden arrow glyph with no text at all.
  • Ambiguous CTA labels get an accessible name that includes the headline. Aldi's button is labelled "Order Now" visually but aria-label="Order Now Get 30% off your first DoorDash order.* (opens in a new tab)". Five links reading "Shop now" are useless in a link list; this is how they fix it without changing the design.
  • Decorative artwork is hidden, and hidden properly. eBay belt-and-braces it with both aria-hidden="true" on the wrapper and an empty alt.
  • Visual size is decoupled from semantic level. Dawn's heading block offers sizes h0, h1, h2, hxl, hxxl while always rendering an <h2> element. Target does the same with styles_fontSize2 on an <h2>.

eBay publishes the rule the others only imply

eBay's Evo design system is the one place I found the whole-tile-link question answered explicitly, and the answer is a binary with no middle:

  • A self-actionable card — the whole tile is one link or button — cannot contain interactive elements such as buttons or links within it. Focus lands only on the card.
  • A non-self-actionable card is inert, "not itself interactive, but houses elements, such as an image wrapped by an anchor, an add to favorites button, item title that is also wrapped in an anchor". Focus lands on the children.
  • Evo also warns against nesting headings or lists inside a self-actionable card, "as screen readers will only announce the button or link text" — which explains IKEA's choice of a <p> rather than an <h2> inside its whole-card link.
  • The hybrid — whole-tile click plus inner controls — is explicitly pushed to a <div> with JavaScript event handling rather than nested anchors.

That resolves the apparent disagreement in the table above. Target and IKEA are self-actionable, so they have exactly one anchor and their "CTA" is decoration. eBay and Aldi are non-self-actionable, so they have named child links and a real button. Both are correct; what is never correct is a whole-tile link with a button inside it, which is the shape most hand-rolled promo tiles drift into. A save/favourite control on a promotional tile forces you into the second camp.

If the tiles are in a carousel, eBay's rules are unusually prescriptive

  • The carousel must have a heading in the page's heading structure, and items must be marked up as a list. Previous/next must be real <button> elements. Peeked partial slides must not be focusable.
  • Items outside the viewport must not be keyboard focusable and must not be reachable with the virtual cursor — navigation is via the buttons only.
  • Auto-rotation requires a pause/play control that is the first interactive element and always visible; it must pause on hover and on keyboard focus entering. Discrete carousels should not auto-start; continuous ones must not.
  • Disabled edge buttons stay in the tab order, announced as disabled — deliberately, so focus is never lost when a control disables under it.
  • Focus order: pause/play → previous → in-viewport content → next → pagination. ARIA: role="group" + aria-roledescription + aria-labelledby pointing at the visible heading.

Target's screen-reader craft, worth stealing

Its display headline renders "Up to 40%" as styled spans, then repairs the reading with <span class="h-sr-only">percent off</span> beside the numeral and aria-hidden="true" on the decorative % and on the <br> that forces the line break. A sighted user sees a stacked display number; a screen reader hears "Up to 40 percent off". Responsive line breaks use <br class="dn"> so they vanish at small sizes.

DoorDash

Food delivery · the "Facet" server-driven UI systemPublished protobuf
Live text
Markup was unobtainable, but DoorDash published the actual protobuf, which is better. A Facet is documented as mapping "one-to-one to a view on the screen" — and the homepage rewrite that motivated it was explicitly about supporting "non-restaurant merchants, such as grocery stores, flower shops, convenience stores".
Facet {
  id, component { id, category }, text, images, custom, events,
  children[]           // recursive
  logging, layout, style
}
Text   { title, subtitle, accessory, description, custom{} }
Images { main, icon, background, accessory, custom{} }
Image  { uri, placeholder, local, style: STYLE_ROUNDED | STYLE_CIRCLE }
Events { click -> Action { name, data } }
FacetSection { id, header[], body[], layout }
The key design idea
The text and image sub-models are semantic slots, not domain fields. Each is documented as "means different things in the context of different components". title/subtitle/accessory/description is DoorDash's answer to eyebrow/headline/subcopy, and images.main/background/accessory is its answer to artwork versus badge.
Worked example
text: {title: "Frosty Bear", subtitle: "Pizza, Vegetarian, DashPass", description: "4.8, 14,400+ ratings, Free delivery over $12", annotation: "$10 off, DashPass only"} with images: {main, background, accessory: "dashpass-logo"}. Note the promotional line arrives as an annotation — a copy slot, not a discount object.
Lessons they published
Three worth having. The custom escape hatch is named as an anti-pattern by its own authors — engineers reach for it instead of the prototypical fields. Prototypical fallback components were built and abandoned as useless. Client capability versioning (SemVer on the request, plus dropping containers with no recognised children and Facets with unknown actions) "reduced our need to version our libraries by about 50%".
Cost
19 net-new components per platform, three client engineers each on iOS and Android. GraphQL could not express the recursive model, so responses are flattened server-side with a childrenCount and rebuilt on the client.

What the evidence supports

If you are building this once, build it this way

  • Live text, always. Baked-in copy appears once in eleven surfaces, in one full-bleed slot, at a company with a studio on staff. Everything else is structured fields. It also settles your translation, dark-mode and responsive-type problems for free.
  • Default the text to below or beside the image; make overlay the opt-in. That is Dawn's default, Aldi's only mode, and eBay's and Instacart's hero pattern. It is also the only mode that cannot fail against an unpredictable photograph.
  • When you must overlay, use a fixed-height eased band, not a full-tile wash. IKEA's thirteen stops over 200px is copy-pasteable and is the single highest-value artefact here.
  • Prefer a named colour scheme to per-field hex. Instacart and eBay reference token names; Walmart and Target let merchandisers type hex and now have #FAf0E6 and #FAF0E6 in the same page. For a monochrome system this matters more, not less — a hex field is how a hue gets in.
  • Make tile size authored data with a semantic name. Walmart's verticalSmallLeft beats an array index because the artwork brief, the asset filename and the slot all agree. IKEA's container queries mean one component serves every size.
  • Give the disclaimer its own field and its own optional URL. Three of four schemas have one; asterisks in headlines with nowhere to resolve are the alternative, and Walmart demonstrates the failure mode.
  • Let the CDN negotiate the format. Six of seven retail sites ship no format branch at all — one URL per rendition and Vary: Accept. Only Uber Eats emits an explicit <source type="image/webp">, and it gains nothing a negotiating CDN doesn't already give you.
  • Carry the tile's foreground colour in the payload, derived from the artwork. Uber Eats' isBackgroundDark is the cleanest solution found to the problem an inverting accent creates: the photograph doesn't invert when the theme does, so the type colour over it must not be a theme token. Author it with the creative, and leave it alone in dark mode.
  • Give the CTA its own colour pair, independent of the card's. Uber Eats does; it is what lets a pill stay legible when it sits over a different region of the image from the headline.
  • Ship accessibilityText beside visible badge text, not instead of it. "Top Offer • Buy 1, Get 1 Free" needs a spoken form; a bullet character read aloud is noise.
  • If you ever bake a field, bake only that field, and restate it in the DOM. Woolworths bakes the price for typographic control and puts an sr-only copy beside it. Amazon bakes everything and has to duplicate the headline into alt and aria-label — that is the cost, and it is visible in their markup.
  • Clamp the copy. eBay's line-clamp-3/line-clamp-2 is the only defence in the sample against a merchandiser pasting a paragraph into a headline field.
  • Decide the tile's link structure before you build it, using eBay's binary. One anchor around the whole tile and no interactive children, or an inert tile with named child controls. If a promo tile will ever carry a save control, you are in the second camp and the CTA must be a real button with an accessible name that includes the headline.
  • Don't call it Banner. In every design system I could read, that word already means a system alert. PromoTile, Teaser (Aldi/AEM), Storycard (Target) or TextOverlayCard (IKEA) all read unambiguously.
  • If the tiles sit in a carousel, the first slide owns your LCP. fetchpriority="high" on slide one and nothing else; never lazy-load it. Chrome still counts an image as an LCP candidate after it has been removed from the DOM, so an auto-rotating carousel can hand its LCP to whichever slide happens to be heaviest.
